What You Have
Architecture: You've built a Julia DSL that defines PK models as equation systems with a custom @model macro, covering both static (NonlinearSolve) and dynamic (discrete-time Newton-Raphson) solvers, with balance sheet accounting and a Dash web dashboard for interactive exploration. The code infrastructure is genuinely impressive — the DSL is clean, well-structured, and makes it easy to iterate on model variants.

Model variants implemented (static):
1. PQ — asset market added, no real feedback (pure balance sheet inflation)
2. PQC — credit rationing (c) depends on asset demand (AD) (crowding out)
3. PQCr — policy rate responds to asset prices (i = i_0 + i_1 P + i_2 AP)
4. PQCrDIFF — dual interest rates for speculative vs. productive lending
5. With endogenized α — asset turnover responds to price

Dynamic models: Various lag structures, adaptive expectations in SD, endogenized α and c with time dependence, and the beginnings of a hysteresis approach.

Novel Elements
1. The turnover rate as a bridging mechanism. The parameters (\alpha) and (\gamma) — governing what fraction of the asset stock is traded and how speculative debt feeds into asset demand — are the most interesting conceptual contribution. The idea that the scale mismatch between speculative transactions and GDP is regulated by a turnover multiplier is something you explicitly reference in the notes: "our argument on turnover implicitly creates a multiplier that governs the mismatch in scale between speculative transactions and GDP (also already discussed in the Treatise!)." This is genuinely worth developing. If you can formalize a "turnover multiplier" analogous to the income multiplier, that would be a real contribution.

2. The staged model architecture (PQ → PQC → PQCr → PQCrDIFF). This is pedagogically excellent — each variant adds exactly one feedback channel, making it easy to see which assumptions drive which results. This is your strongest selling point for a teaching note.

3. The Treatise → General Theory framing. The idea of using the shift from the Treatise (industrial vs. financial circulation as separate) to the General Theory (liquidity preference as a unified theory linking speculation to real activity) as a pedagogical device to motivate the model variants is clever and, as far as I can tell, hasn't been done in this form. The PQ model corresponds to the Treatise view (speculation inflates balance sheets but has no real impact), while PQC/PQCr correspond to the General Theory view (speculation crowds out productive credit and/or affects the interest rate). This is a nice way to make the history of thought do work in the exposition rather than being a decorative aside.

4. Dual interest rate policy. The (i_{AP}) parameter in PQCrDIFF — a penalty on speculative financing — is a policy-relevant extension that connects to current debates on macroprudential policy and "leaning against the wind."

What the Literature Does Better
1. Stock-Flow Consistent (SFC) models. This is the big one. Your balance sheet approach is SFC-like but much more rudimentary. Godley & Lavoie (2007) Monetary Economics already integrates asset markets, speculation, and real-financial linkages with full accounting consistency across sectoral balance sheets. The "asset market" you've built (AD, AS, AP) is a much simpler price mechanism than the portfolio choice models in SFC (Tobin's q, dividend-arbitrage pricing, etc.).
