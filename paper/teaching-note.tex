\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

While Keynes is often credited with fundamentally changing the way economists think about money and finance, especially when it comes to the evaluating the link between financial market dynamics and real economic activity.
However, Keynes himself was not fully consistent in his treatment of money, finance and speculation across his works and in the past decades different authors and traditions took up and emphasized different aspects of his core contributions.
This leads to a fragmented reception of Keynes' ideas on money and finance, where later works often highlight specific takes advanced by Keynes, but fail to provide a coherent picture of his overall view.
In this paper we build on \citet{fontanaSimpleTeachableMacroeconomic2009} to develop a simple model that synthesizes Keynes's key insights from an endogenous money perspective. The model illustrates shows how different assumptions about the nature of the financial sector and role of speculative activity determine whether and to what extent financial aspects shape real economic outcomes.

Aside from its core purpose to rationally reconstruct key stances on money and finance developed by Keynes, we find that the resulting model can also be used to introduce students to the concept of endogenous money as a key starting point for understanding and modeling interactions between real and financial sectors.

To also provide a historically coherent account, we first trace Keynes' original arguments in the \treatise and the \GT in section \ref{sec:historyofthought} and discuss how Keynes used these different approaches to describe monetary policy and financial activities under different historical and institutional conditions. Following the Keynesian adage that economists should focusing on "choosing models that are relevant to the contemporary world" \citep{Keynes1938HarrodLetter}, we suggest recombining different arguments made in both works to arrive at a convincing representations of contemporary monetary systems.

On this basis, we then present a simple baseline model in section \ref{sec:model} that provides a framework for integrating key insights of Keynes on money and speculation. We then enrich this baseline model in several ways to illustrate how speculative markets may impact on the real economy and how different model assumptions can capture different channels of influence that have been proposed in the respective literature (see section \ref{sec:real_impact}). Finally, we aim to connect our modeling efforts with real-world experiences relating in economic policy and speculative practice in section \ref{sec:real_world} with a topical focus of notions on stability and instability.

\section{Keynes on money and speculation} \label{sec:historyofthought}

It is a quite well-known fact  that the \GT and the \treatise do rest on fundamentally different conceptions of \emph{money itself} \parencite[e.g.][]{smithinKeynessTheoriesMoney2013, patinkinKeynesMonetaryThought1976, roncagliaBriefHistoryEconomic2017}.

The \treatise is more preoccupied with the mechanics of credit creation and provides a detailed classification of how bank credit eventually creates bank deposits so that the \treatise is effectively driven by a view of money as \emph{endogenous}: the banking system creates purchasing power through lending, and the money supply expands with bank credit. This is a credit-based rather than a horizontalist form of endogeneity: banks ration credit in line with their own liquidity and risk concerns, and the central bank continues to steer the quantity of money through the bank rate \parencite{carvalhoKeynesEndogeneityMoney2013}. The \GT, in contrast, shelves this institutional apparatus. Money enters the analysis as a simple stock of base money $M=\bar{M}$, determined by the monetary authority, and the analytical effort shifts entirely to the demand side---the liquidity preference schedule. The banking system fades into the background; what matters is the portfolio choice between holding money and holding assets \parencite{chickMacroeconomicsKeynesReconsideration1983}.

Such portfolio choice thereby also touches on the role of speculation, as portfolios might also cover more speculative assets, that are held not because of their productive capacities and associated economic return, but because their owners expect (or, hope for) an increase of their underlying market price.
Speculation is, thereby, distinct from investment as it channels liquidity into purposes that do not facilitate further economic activity. The fact that speculative activity does not enter GDP directly often motivates bracketing out speculative behavior completely in both, teaching and research contexts. 

However, which exact relationship exists between financial speculation and real economic activity is one of the oldest questions in monetary theory.
Again, within Keynes's own oeuvre, two different answers can be found.
In the \treatise{}, originally published in 1930, speculative activity is largely cordoned off from the real economy: money used for financial transactions circulates in a different ``sphere'' from money used for production and consumption. In the \GT{} from 1936, however, this cordon is removed: speculative motives are captured by the notion of a liquidity preference. As a consequence, asset price movements originating in financial markets affect the demand for money, which in turn can affect the rate of interest, and thereby investment, output, and employment.

The shift from the \treatise{} to the \GT{} is therefore not a single change but a \emph{double shift}: one along the speculation--real-economy dimension, another along the endogenous--exogenous money dimension.
These two shifts can be represented by the simple typology, shown in Table~\ref{tab:typology}.

The four cells of Table~\ref{tab:typology} represent four logically coherent positions.
In the top left corner money is as ``neutral'' as it goes: here, base money is a policy-determined stock ($\bar{M}$) and financial markets are an intermediating sideshow that do not affect real activity. Monetary policy, however, does matter as it determines the rate of interest (either directly or via $\bar{M}$). In the top right corner, in contrast, speculative motives may influence the rate of interest directly and, hence, allow for a feedback of asset prices on real economic developments. Here, Keynes asserts that when asset prices are high, investors will tend to hold money -- the liquidity preference -- which drives up interest rates, and vice versa.

In contrast, the \treatise conventionally proceeds on the assumption that such speculative activity, while relevant to for financial stability in an endogenous money context, does not directly impact on credit provided for investment (or consumption) purposes.
Hence, as indicated in the bottom left cell of table \ref{tab:typology}, the link between speculation and real economic developments is absent and the focus is directed on the internal dynamics of the banking sector and the inflation and contraction of balance sheets of financial market actors. 

Finally, the bottom-right bracket poses a scenario, where we allow for both, endogenous money and real-world impacts of speculative motives and tendencies.
In principle, such an account resonates well with a Post-Keynesian stance, which is typically associated with both, the assumption of endogenous money and the conviction that financial markets are not neutral intermediaries. However, at the same time models in the Post-Keynesian tradition often avoid making the impact of speculation explicit, because modeling the impact of speculation on GDP and employment requires the introduction of specific assumptions that make this link explicit, which has the potential to substantially complicate otherwise simple model setups.\footnote{Explicit counterexamples include \citet{erturkMacroeconomicsSpeculation2005}, who links biased asset-price expectations to output in a \treatise-inspired monetary model; \citet{ryooLongWavesShort2010} and \citet{ryooHouseholdDebtHousing2016}, who model equity-price and housing-price cycles, respectively; and \citet{grasselliInflationSpeculationDynamic2015}, who add a stock-flow-consistent flow of credit for purchases of existing financial assets to a monetary Keen model. Morevoer, see \citet{nikolaidiMinskyModelsStructured2017} for a survey of Minskyan models in which asset prices play a central role.}

In section \ref{sec:model} further below we provide a simple model that focuses on the bottom row of table \ref{tab:typology} and compares different variants of bringing endogenous money in a simple modeling framework. This framework makes  the different channels and arguments, by which speculative behavior might indeed impact on the development of the real economy, explicit.
However, before doing so we revisit key instances in Keynes' original works to better substantiate our summary as well as the successive modeling efforts.

\subsection{Keynes on endogenous vs.\ exogenous Money} \label{subsec:endogenous-money}

The \treatise{} develops one of the most meticulous accounts of the origins and nature of credit-money in the history of economic thought, effectively emphasizing that banks create money (in the form of deposits) by providing credit, that is then channeled back to their own accounts.
In principle, this money creation by private banks can operate stable, without limit when risks within the banking sector remain fairly evenly distributed. The \treatise[Vol.~1, Ch.~2] summarizes this stance in a famous statement on the endogenous money principle:

\begin{quote}
There is no limit to the amount of bank money which the banks can safely create provided that they move forward in step. \citep[Vol.~1, Ch.~2, p.~23]{johnmaynardkeynesTreatiseMoneyTwo2011}
\end{quote}

Keynes elaborates on this in the \treatise[Vol.~1, Ch.~3] by classifying bank deposits into two main categories according to their economic function: income-deposits (households' transaction balances) and business-deposits (firms' working capital) for transactions to ensure daily production and provisioning, and savings-deposits (interest-bearing savings accounts) as well deposits for financial transactions of firm dedicate to organize financial investments. The classification is reproduced in Table~\ref{tab:treatise-balance} and summarized in \treatise[Vol.~1, Ch.~15] under two broader headings, where real and financial circulation a are separated into two different spheres.

\begin{table}[h]

The detailed taxonomy itself embodies a theory of money creation. Deposits are not simply ``there'' in a fixed quantity; they are endogenous to the lending activities of the banking system. Deposits corresponding to the \textit{industrial circulation} are created when banks extend credit for financing production (or consumption), while deposits associated with \textit{financial circulation} are created when banks lend for financial transactions or when households decide to hold idle balances. The total quantity of money is determined by the interaction of bank lending decisions and the public's willingness to hold deposits. There is no \emph{exogenous} money stock --- the central bank influences the process through the bank rate and open-market operations, but it does not set the quantity of money directly.

In the \GT[Ch.~15], this institutional apparatus is set aside. Keynes defines money demand and introduces introduces the liquidity preference function as:

\begin{equation}
M = M_1 + M_2 = L_1(Y) + L_2(r) \tag{GT, Ch.~15}
\end{equation}

The left-hand side $M$ is the total quantity of money, now treated as a variable ``determined by the action of the central bank'' \citep[Ch.~15]{Keynes.2018} --- that is, as an exogenously given stock. The analytical effort shifts entirely to the right-hand side: the \emph{demand} for money, which is decomposed into the transactions motive $L_1(Y)$ and the speculative motive $L_2(r)$. The latter variable is thereby directly tied to asset prices, as the real interest rate $r$ is high when bond prices are low, and vice versa.

As large parts of the \GT treat bonds as the main category of speculative assets, the interest rate $r$ and the price of speculative assets behave proportionally: the greater the price of speculative assets, the higher the demand for money (as people wait for falling asset prices), which effectively drives the price of cash (in form of $r$). While such a channel from speculative to interest rate seems plausible in principle -- e.g., when central banks respond to speculative frenzies by financial market tightening --, this setups results in a very peculiar and specific scenario, where speculative dynamics are the sole main driver of real interest rates. 

However, at the same time, high real interest rates might easily feed back on asset prices -- e.g., via lack of cheap credit to sustain high asset prices --, an aspect that is not directly incorporated in the above setup of the \GT. So while there seems to be some consensus that simultaneity is at work, when it comes to the relationship between interest rates and asset prices, the \GT provides a very specific interpretation of this causal channel: it effectively abstracts from both, other forces impacting on the interest rate as well as possible feedback effects from the interest rate on asset prices. This specific construction, somewhat ironically, actually limits the generality of the \GT, which does not model the interplay of interest rates and asset prices in great detail. In contrast, the treatment found in the \treatise offers a more open and flexible starting point to model this simultaneous relationship.

A further consequence of this assumption is that the banking system as a credit-creating institution is essentially absent from the analysis in the \GT; loans, deposits, and reserve management are no longer explicitly modeled.Consequently, the famous classification in the \treatise[Vol.~1, Ch.~3] is collapsed into a single sentence in the \GT[Ch.~15]:

\begin{quote}
Money held for each of the three purposes forms, nevertheless, a single pool, which the holder is under no necessity to segregate into three water-tight compartments; for they need not be sharply divided even in his own mind, and the same sum can be held primarily for one purpose and secondarily for another. \citep[p. 172]{Keynes.2018}
\end{quote}

The ``single pool'' formulation is an important analytical simplification --- it lets Keynes model portfolio choice parsimoniously --- but it also discards the \treatise's insight that the money stock is itself an outcome of the credit creation process.
In sum, the \treatise{} asks more macroscopic question ``how is money created?'' and answers ``through bank lending,'', while the \GT{} poses the more decision-focused question ``how is money held?'' and answers ``as a portfolio choice between liquidity and earning assets.'' In the second version of the \GT macro-properties of the monetary system are then presented as a corrollary to that questions. While both questions provide legitimate vantage points in principle, they are anchored in different theoretical perspectives as represented --- the left and right columns of the typology shown in table \ref{tab:typology}.

\subsection{The Real Impacts of Speculation} \label{subsec:speculation}

The second cleavage identified when comparing the \treatise and the \GT concerns the question, whether speculative activity in financial markets can affect real economic activity, that is, output, employment, or productivity. Here again the two books give different answers. 

As already indicated by \ref{tab:treatise-balance{}}, Keynes divides the total money stock into two distinct \emph{circulations} in the \treatise that can, in principle, operate without limits and, hence, expand and contract independently as long as they remain internally stable.
The distinction is drawn in the \treatise[Vol.~1, Ch.~15]:

\begin{quote}
\[I\]t is necessary to make [...] a division between the deposits used for the purposes of industry, which we shall call the \emph{Industrial Circulation}, and those used for the purposes of Finance, which we shall call the \emph{Financial Circulation}. By \emph{Industry} we mean the business of maintaining the normal process of current output, distribution and exchange [...]. By \emph{Finance}, on the other hand, we mean the business of holding and exchanging existing titles to wealth. \citep[Vol.~1, Ch.~15, p.~217]{johnmaynardkeynesTreatiseMoneyTwo2011}
\end{quote}

As money is used for financial transactions circulates in a different loop from money used for production, then an expansion of the financial circulation --- driven by speculative lending --- need not touch the industrial circulation at all. Keynes makes this very explicit in the \treatise[Vol.~1, Ch.~17] and thereby also integrates the observation that speculative activity will not, as such, contribute to investment and, hence, GDP will remain unaffected from such speculative activity.

\begin{quote}
[R]ising prices of securities will cause a difference of opinion to develop between two sections of the financial community,---one section (the ``bulls'') believing in the continuance of the price-rise and willing to buy with borrowed money, the other section (the ``bears'') doubting the continuance and preferring to sell securities for cash or bills...
So long as the new money is absorbed [...], without, however, stimulating the output of new investment, there will be no effect on the purchasing power of money. \citep[Vol.~1, Ch.~17, pp.~239--240]{johnmaynardkeynesTreatiseMoneyTwo2011}
\end{quote}

This is the \emph{cordon sanitaire}: speculative activity can expand the financial circulation indefinitely --- the bull--bear dynamic visible in financial markets absorbs new credit without transmitting it to the industrial circulation --- and real activity remains unchanged.

In the \GT{}, this separation is abandoned. The same money stock serves both the transactions and speculative motives, as the liquidity preference equation $M = L_1(Y) + L_2(r)$ makes clear. Now as these two motives draw on a common pool, any increase in speculative demand for money also presses on the rate of interest, which creates a link between speculation and the real economy:

\begin{quote}
It is by playing on the speculative-motive that monetary management (or, in the absence of management, chance changes in the quantity of money) is brought to bear on the economic system. \citep[p. 173]{Keynes.2018}
\end{quote}

However, Keynes acknowledges the simultaneous nature of the speculation---interest-rate nexus and, especially, the fact that speculation als has an impact of the rate of interest, without making this complex recursive structure explicit in this modeling setup.\footnote{The most straightforward way to express this in the framework of the \GT would be to state $M = M_Y + M_r = L_1(Y) + L_2(r(M_2))$, which makes the recursive character explicit and would invite a more explicit consideration of time as also found in the simple model shown in section \ref{sec:model}.}.
However, this recursive structure is nonetheless decisive for understanding the feedback on macroeconomic dynamics as the interest rate also governs investment, and investment governs output and employment by means of the consumption multiplier. Hence, the channel from speculation to the real economy in the \GT explicitly runs through the cost of capital, not through balance-sheet crowding-out. And a famous passage from the \GT[Ch.~12] captures the \emph{normative} implication:

\begin{quote}
Speculators may do no harm as bubbles on a steady stream of enterprise. But the position is serious when enterprise becomes the bubble on a whirlpool of speculation. When the capital development of a country becomes a by-product of the activities of a casino, the job is likely to be ill-done. \citep[p. 139]{Keynes.2018}
\end{quote}

Speculation, hence, can harm enterprise and economic development, in the \GT{} framework, \textit{only through the rate of interest}. A more leveraged financial sector is fine as long as it does not shift the liquidity preference schedule too much so that productive investment is left unconstrained. In such a situation speculation is, strictly speaking, harmless. However, as soon as speculation may crowd out investment via higher interest rates this separation breaks and speculation can have \textit{real} consequences.\footnote{The rate of interest is thereby not the only possible dampening channel on investment. A key example for an alternative factor is that speculation provide an outside option for liquid individuals, who can directly substitute investment for speculative activity.}

\section{A simple teaching model} \label{sec:model}

In what follows we embed key arguments from the bottom row of table \ref{tab:typology} in a simple linear model of endogenous money.
We thereby take the endogenous money model by \citet{fontanaSimpleTeachableMacroeconomic2009} as a starting point and extend this model with an explicit market for speculative assets.

The model presentation draws on the exposition by \citet{DIYMacroeconomicModel} and has been implemented in Julia. We start by revisiting the key structure of the model, before adding the asset market module in a second step.\footnote{The respective package is available on \href{https://github.com/theplatters/PKAssetPrices}{Github}. It starts from the model representation as given by \citet{DIYMacroeconomicModel} reproduces \href{https://macrosimulation.org/a_post_keynesian_macro_model_with_endogenous_money}{the respective model} and successively extends the framework to provide a simplified representation of asset market dyanmics and their interaction with the real economy.}


\subsection{The basic model}\label{subsec:basic_model}
The real side of the model is described by a goods market and a wage-price system to represent labor market dynamics. The monetary side, in contrast, is governed by an endogenous money creation process and a Taylor-style policy rule. While being modeled as an endogenous variable, the interest rate depends on a series of parameters that are (exogenously) set by the central bank, so that different impacts on interest rate dynamics can be modeled in such a setup.

The key equations for the goods market are, hence, given by 

\paragraph{Goods market I}
\begin{align}
  Y &= \ND + c D \label{eq:simple_y}, \\
  \ND &= b Y \label{eq:simple_nd}, \\
  D &= d_0 - d_1 r \label{eq:simple_d},
\end{align}

where output $Y$ is the sum of non-debt-financed demand $\ND$ and credit-rationed debt-financed demand $cD$ with $c$ serving as a credit rationing parameter.
As non-debt-financed demand depends on income (Eq.~\ref{eq:simple_nd}) and debt-financed demand depends negatively on the lending rate $r$(Eq.~\ref{eq:simple_d}), there is a close conceptual correspondence between $\ND$ and consumption ($C$ in conventional models) as well as $cD$ and $I$, where the former can be interpreted as credit lines dedicated to finance real investments (with $(1-c)$ giving the share of planned investments $I_P$ that could not be implement due to a lack of liquid funds).

Against this backdrop we would suggest to rewrite equations \ref{eq:simple_y} in teaching contexts as

\paragraph{Goods market II}
\begin{align}
  Y &= C + I = \ND + cD \label{eq:teaching_y}, \\
  \ND &= C = b Y \label{eq:teaching_nd}, \\
  D &= I_P = d_0 - d_1 r \label{eq:teaching_d},
\end{align}

to better align the presentation with conventional notation and assumptions typically employed in Keynesian-style models.
This applies especially to the idea that consumption is governed by comparatively constant behavioral patterns (see equation \ref{eq:teaching_nd}), while investment is guided by animal spirits and financial markets conditions.
In the above setup, the latter enter via $r$ as a general constraint and $c$, which depends on the portfolio choices of the banking sector as well as the solvency of potential borrowers.\footnote{In Minskyan terms, $c$ can be interpreted as a reduced-form representation of borrower's and lender's risk, which constrain the volume of debt-financed investment \parencite[Ch.~8]{minskyStabilizingUnstableEconomy1986}. While $c$ is taken as given in the baseline model, Section~\ref{subsec:banks_ration} subsequently endogenizes it with respect to asset demand. The model nevertheless remains static and therefore does not capture the intertemporal transition from hedge to speculative and Ponzi finance central to Minsky's financial instability hypothesis \parencite{minskyFinancialInstabilityHypothesis1992}.}

As already indicated the monetary sector is mainly governed by interest rates $r$, which are jointly determined by the central bank's policy rate $i$ as well as bank markup $m$. The remaining equations do collect relevant transactions for accounting purposes.

\paragraph{Monetary system}
\begin{align}
  i &= i_0 + i_1 P \label{eq:simple_i} \\
  r &= (1 + m) i \label{eq:simple_r} \\
  dL &= cD \label{eq:simple_dl} \\
  dM &= dL \label{eq:simple_dm} \\
  dR &= k dM \label{eq:simple_dr}
\end{align}

This model features a minimal version of a Taylor-rule that neglects the output/employment term and focuses on price stability only. Similar to the rationale of the original exposition of \citet{fontanaSimpleTeachableMacroeconomic2009},this setup aims to implement a minimal, but plausible representation of the inner workings of contemporary monetary systems \citep[e.g.][]{mcleayMoneyCreationModern}; appropriate extensions covering this aspect can be easily added in later stages of the exposition.

The accounting scheme underlying this model is still taking reserves into account. However, these reserves are not a constraint on the monetary system, but, rather, arise as an outcome residual from the operation of private banks. Specifically, new loans $dL$ create new deposits $dM$ (Eqs.~\ref{eq:simple_dl}--\ref{eq:simple_dm}) and a fraction $k$ of these deposits -- the reserve rate -- is generated as reserves $dR$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:simple_dr}) by the central bank. Money is, hence, purely endogenous as the supply of credit determines the money stock. While our focus is on models of endogenous money that seem to bear greater relevance for the description of contemporary economies, \textit{reserves} are the obvious anchor for bringing exogenous notions of money back into the story.

The final component of this minimal macroeconomic model with endogenous money is given by the labor market conditions -- employment $N$ and unemployment $U$ -- and corresponding trends in wages $W$ and prices $P$.

\paragraph{Labor markets and wage-price nexus}
\begin{align}
  P &= (1 + n) a W \label{eq:simple_p} \\
  W &= W_0 - h U \label{eq:simple_w} \\
  N &= a Y \label{eq:simple_n} \\
  U &= 1 - \frac{N}{N^f} \label{eq:simple_u}
\end{align}

Here we reproduce a simple standard setup: firms set prices as a markup on unit labour costs (Eq.~\ref{eq:simple_p}), while nominal wages fall with unemployment (Eq.~\ref{eq:simple_w}), reflecting workers' reduced bargaining power. In this setup, $a$ is a fixed unit labour requirement, so that productivity is given by $\frac{1}{a}$. Employment is therefore proportional to output (Eq.~\ref{eq:simple_n}) and unemployment is given by the gap between labour supply $N^f$ and actual employment (Eq.~\ref{eq:simple_u}).

Figure~\ref{fig:simplepk-equilibrium} summarizes the baseline formulation diagrammatically by showing how a change in interest rates affect the static quilibrium. \subsection{Adding an Asset Market}\label{subsec:asset_market}

In a next step we introduce a market for speculative assets in this framework. Four new variables describe it: nominal speculative debt $SD$, nominal gross asset expenditure $AE$, the quantity of assets offered for trade $QA$, and the level of asset prices $AP$. The assets can be interpreted as urban real estate, bonds, shares, pieces of art, crypto-assets, or a composite of these. As a consequence our conceptualization of assets covers everything that can be conceived to targeted for speculative endeavors and is not per se restricted to the financial sphere. Because Eq.~\ref{eq:as} pins $QA$ to parameters, the operative feedback among $SD$, $AE$, and $AP$ is linear. This keeps the extension analytically transparent while allowing the asset market and the macroeconomic block to be solved simultaneously.


\paragraph{Asset markets}
\begin{align}
  SD &= s_0-s_1r-s_2(AP-1), \qquad s_1,s_2>0, \label{eq:sd} \\
  AE &= \frac{SD}{1-\gamma}, \qquad 0\leq\gamma<1, \label{eq:ad} \\
  AP &= \frac{AE}{QA}, \label{eq:ap} \\
  QA &= \bar{QA} \qquad \bar{QA} > 0 . \label{eq:as}
\end{align}

The asset-price index is normalized so that $AP=1$ denotes its reference level.
Consequently, $s_0$ is speculative debt at the reference asset price and a zero lending rate;
more generally, debt taken is $s_0-s_1 r - s_2(AP-1)$: Speculative debt falls with the lending rate, because higher financing costs discourage leveraged positions, and with the current asset price. The latter represents the intuition of the ``liquidity preference'': as assets become expensive, a given nominal position buys less and speculators become more inclined to hold liquidity. Conversely, an asset price below one raises speculative borrowing. While this setup correpsonds to the notion of a liquidity preference, it differs from the \GT{} bond-market construction, however, where the lending rate and the asset-price deviation are directly related (as bonds serve as the main asset type). In contrast, these variables enter Eq.~\ref{eq:sd} as distinct, independent arguments. 

Buying assets shifts liquidity to their sellers. If a share $\gamma$ of each seller's receipts is used to purchase another asset within the same period, repeated transactions generate the multiplier $1/(1-\gamma)$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:ad}. Thus $AE$ measures gross nominal transactions - in contrast to either newly created bank debt ($SD$) or a physical stock of assets ($QA$).



Equations~\ref{eq:sd}--\ref{eq:ap} form a simultaneous but linear feedback loop. Substitution gives the unique asset price
\begin{equation}
  AP
  =\frac{\left[ (1 - \gamma) + (s_0+s_2-s_1r)\right]}
  {QA(1-\gamma) +s_2},
  \label{eq:linear-ap-solution}
\end{equation}
provided $QA>0$ and $0\leq\gamma<1$. The negative price-deviation term in speculative debt raises the denominator and therefore dampens, rather than amplifies, the contemporaneous price response. 

This simple static model gives only a stylized representation and does not represent the full spectrum of forces influencing speculative demand. In particular, it contains the deviation of the current asset-price index from its normalized reference level but neither past nor expected price changes. One may interpret $\gamma$ as a reduced-form indicator of market sentiment: expected price increases encourage sellers to reinvest more of their receipts, whereas pessimism reduces this within-period recycling. Such an interpretation resonates with the \treatise{} emphasis on bullish and bearish expectations discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:speculation}, but expectations remain exogenous in the present model. However, from the perspective of the \GT{}, $\gamma$ can be interpreted as a reduced-form representation of the liquidity preference function: here, $\gamma$ captures the degree to which agents are willing to hold onto the receipts from sold assets for future investment versus reinvesting them immediately.

AS indicated, speculative debt now appears as a liability of the private sector and
an asset of the banking system alongside productive loans $cD$. Consequently,
$dM=dL$ also records deposits created to finance speculative positions, as in
Keynes's claim that bank-money expansion accommodates the financial circulation.

\begin{equation}
  dL = cD + SD \label{eq:dl-extended} 
\end{equation}

This variant of the model corresponds precisely to the \treatise{} position when taking $c=c_0$ as a fixed exogenous parameter. Then asset market dynamics as represented by \(SD\), \(AE\), \(AP\), \(QA\) inflate the balance sheet (loans and deposits both grow by \(SD\)), but none of the real variables are affected; they are fully contained within the financial circulation and do not feed back into the industrial circulation. Hence, output, employment, the price level, and the interest rate are identical to the baseline model without an asset market.

Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-equilibrium} displays this separation graphically. In this simple model setup, the real interest rate $r$ is the key variable that has an impact on both, real and financial, markets as lower interest not only drive up real investment, but also make speculative endeavors less risky. This increases the net demand for assets when interest rates are reduced as depicted in the far right panel in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-equilibrium}. The balance sheet  in the lower panel of the same figure indicates how the introduction of an asset-market inflates sector balance sheets due to the presence of speculative borrowing. However, this balance sheet inflation does not carry any real economic consequences as Keynes wrote, speculative expansion of bank money can be contained within the financial circulation: ``there will be no effect on the purchasing power of money'' \citep[Vol.~1, Ch.~17, pp.~270--271]{johnmaynardkeynesTreatiseMoneyTwo2011}.

To assess the proportion of newly created bank money that is absorbed by the asset market we introduce the risk indicator $\psi := \frac{SD}{dL} = \frac{SD}{cD + SD}$, with $1-\psi$ giving the productive share of new lending. Because loans create deposits one-for-one in this framework ($dM=dL$), $\psi$ measures the share of new money creation that enters the financial rather than the industrial circulation; it thereby provides a quantitative counterpart to the deposit classification of the \treatise{} reproduced in Table~\ref{tab:treatise-balance}. The indicator is reported in Figures \ref{fig:baseline-equilibrium}, \ref{fig:rationing-equilibrium}, \ref{fig:centralbank-equilibrium}, \ref{fig:firmsration-equilibrium}
and \ref{fig:pqcrdiff-equilibrium} for the corresponding scenarios.

Two lessons follow. First, since $\partial SD/\partial r<0$, monetary easing mechanically raises $\psi$ even when real outcomes remain unchanged. Cheap credit tilts the composition of new lending toward speculation, mirroring the risk-taking channel documented for the post-2020 period in Section~\ref{subsec:uncertainty}. Second, $\psi$ is a ratio of flows and captures the orientation of new credit within a period, but not the accumulated leverage that governs financial fragility in the Minskyan sense. It should therefore be read as an indicator of where new money creation is directed, rather than as a sufficient statistic for systemic risk.

To complement the composition indicator $\psi$, we can also gauge the scale of money creation relative to real activity. Defining the money creation ratio
\begin{equation}
  \mu := \frac{dM}{Y} = \frac{dL}{Y},
  \label{eq:mu}
\end{equation}
with loans creating deposits one-for-one ($dM=dL$), $\mu$ measures the flow of newly created bank money per unit of output in the period. Where $\psi$ tells us where new credit is directed (financial vs.\ industrial circulation), $\mu$ tells us how much new money is generated relative to the size of the real economy. A rising $\mu$ therefore signals that an increasing volume of monetary expansion accompanies each unit of GDP. This can happen either through an expansion of the debt-financed investemnts $cD$ or through more speculative debt $SD$.

\section{Real economic impacts of financial speculation} \label{sec:real_impact}

In this section we suggest different formulations that will break the 'cordon sanitaire' betwen industrial and financial circulation and, hence, allow speculation to affect real economic activity. We thereby move from the position of the \treatise{} as captured in section \ref{subsec:asset_market} to a formulation that combines this setup with the idea from the \GT{} that financial transmarkets also have an impact on the real economy. As indicated by table \ref{tab:typology} we thereby move from a rational reconstruction \citep{rortyHistoriographyPhilosophyFour1984} of the \treatise{} to a model rortyHistoriographyPhilosophyFour1984riographyPhilosophyFour1984ng the core intuition found in post-Keynesian (and large parts of heterodox economics).

However, the relevant literature suggests that such interaction between real and financial sphere can be driven by different actors and, hence, different causal channels. In what follows we focus three such channels driven by three different actors. In the first of these the propensity of banks to provide credit for real investment is negatively affected by speculative alternatives mimicing a crowding-out effect in credit provision (section \ref{subsec:banks_ration}). In the second scenario, the central bank is responsive to the development of asset prices $AP$ in addition to the (conventional) price level $P$, which affects the lending rate and, hence, has an indirect impact investment \ref{subsec:central_bank}. Finally, the third scenario captures the idea of a financialization of firms (SOURCE) \ref{subsec:financialization}. In this setup, firms themselves condition their planned productive investment on asset prices taking into account speculative activities as a viable alternative to real investment, which similarly affects real activity \citep{stockhammerFinancialisationSlowdownAccumulation2004,stockhammerFinancialisationDebateTransdisciplinary2026}.


As we will see in what follows these scenarios build on imposing some unilateral impact of financial markets on the real economy; however, developments in the real economy then inevitable feed back on financial markets, which indicates that breaking the cordon sanitaire between both spheres will quickly create non-trivial interactions that depend on the specific modeling setup.

\subsection{The Credit Rationing Channel} \label{subsec:banks_ration}

This channel builds on the intuition that credit provided for financial speculation might crowd out credit provided for real investment \citep{wolfsonPostKeynesianTheory1996a}, e.g. when the former provides higher expected returns or when the related collaterals are perceived as more secure and preferable (e.g. in real-estate). The probably most pronounced example for such an effect is the case of a credit crunch, where a sudden drop in confidence creates a tightening of lending conditions \citep[as in][]{kapellerDebtBoomBust2014}. However, we can also assume a more secular trend, where increased attractivity of speculation requires real investment to provide greater ``margins of safety'' \citep[][Ch. 8]{minskyStabilizingUnstableEconomy1986} and therey constrain access to external funding.

A simple approach to model auch an impact is to partly endogenize the credit rationing parameter $c$ with an endogenous function of asset demand:

\begin{equation}
  c = c_0 - c_1 SD \label{eq:c-sd}
\end{equation}

When speculative debt $SD$ is taken up, the banking sector reduces the share of
credit available for productive purposes. The linear rule is not clipped in
the code, so its economic interpretation requires parameter values that keep
$0\leq c \leq1$.

This yields a \GT{} result: an increase in speculative
activity -- here modeled as a rise in $s_0$ -- raises $SD$, which lowers $c$ thereby contracting demand and, hence, $Y$ through Eqs.~\ref{eq:simple_y} and \ref{eq:simple_nd}.
The mechanism here is a crowding-out effect emerging from the portfolio decisions of banks. Teir impact on speculative leverage is captured by the minimal formulation in equation \ref{eq:c-sd}, but could be modeled in greater detail.

While this setup deviates from the interest rate channel of the
\GT{}, the qualitative conclusion is the same: speculation can
depress real activity. The financial circulation is no longer subject to a cordon
sanitaire---rather, it draws resources away from the industrial circulation.

Figure~\ref{fig:rationing-equilibrium} shows the resulting equilibrium before and after
a change of the baseline interest rate by setting the autonomous interest rate $i_0$ from $1\% $ to $0 \%$. Compared with the Baseline case, speculative demand now lowers available productive credit and therefore shifts the goods-market outcome (panel A in figure \ref{fig:rationing-equilibrium}) downwards, which reduces output and prices as shown in panel B. Asset Dynamics remain close to stable but are subject to a small feedback effect: as decreasing output also comes with lower prices, central banks react via lower interest rates, which slightly amplifies specultive dynamic.\footnote{While small in scale, the emerging feedback effect represents a plausible variant of self-reinforcing economy dynamics: reduced investment leads to lower real economic dynamism, which creates strengthens incentives for speculative activity.}

Finally, the lower left panel summarizes balance sheet changes for the relevant sectors. While the general stock-flow relationships as mapped by tables \ref{tab:sfc-asset-stock} and \ref{tab:sfc-asset-flow} do not change, we observe a slight reduction in the total credit volume as compared by the baseline scenario. This reduction corresponds exactly to the reduction of credit provided for productive investments; correspondingly the share of speculative debt increases slightly.

\subsection{The Central Bank Channel} \label{subsec:central_bank}

In this variant, we assume that the central bank is sensitive to asset price dynamics
and, hence, considers asset prices analogously to prices for goods and services.
Such a setup resonates more directly with Keynes's claim that ``it is by playing
on the speculative-motive that monetary management is brought to bear
on the economic system'': the speculative motive determines asset prices,
which then feed into the policy rate which governs investment. 
Eventually, such a reaction of the central bank can be captured by

\begin{equation}
  i = i_0 + i_1 P + i_2 AP. \label{eq:i-ap}
\end{equation}

Holding the other arguments of the policy rule fixed, a rise in $AP$ raises
the policy rate $i$ and, correspondingly, the lending rate $r$. It therefore reduces both, 
speculative credit $SD$ as well as debt-financed (investment) demand $D$ (as indicated above
by equations \ref{eq:sd} and \ref{eq:teaching_d}).

By couching the mechanism in a modern Taylor-rule-like framework, the chain of
causality now runs different: while in the \GT{} higher asset prices \textit{decrease}
the rate of interest directly (leading people to hold money instead of assets), 
here we assume central banks show sensitivity to asset prive developments and, hecne,
\textit{increase} interest rates. This change occurs because in the \GT{} framework interest 
rates determine the attractiveness of assets, where as in an endogenous money framework
 they impact on their affordability. Hence, such a version would be suitable to embed Keynes this would require that modern central banks would indeed consider asset prices in a systemic, non-discretionary way as suggested by equation \ref{eq:i-ap}. in contrast, much of current central
 bank practice is focused on the consumer price index and largely abstracts from asset price
 developments.


Figure~\ref{fig:centralbank-equilibrium} summarizes the respective model solution, again using the example of a change in the autonomous interest rate from $1\%$ to $0\%$ 
and compares the outcome to the baseline scenario as shown in figure \ref{fig:simplepk-equilibrium} and \ref{fig:baseline-equilibrium}. We immediately find, that the shift in interest rates has a double impact on the goods market as it lowers investment (shift in IS) and increases the baseline interest rate at every price level $P$, which shifts the IR-curve upwards. So, while this policy successfully manages to dampen speculative dynamics and enforce decreasing asset prices, it also leads to a strong drop economic tightening suggesting that such a sensitivity of central banks towards speculation can come with significant real economic costs.

\subsection{The Financialization of Firms} \label{subsec:financialization}

The preceding variants locate the real effects of speculation in the banking
sector or in the policy-rate rule. A further channel arises when firms prioritize the acquisition of speculative assets relative to producive investment.
Such patterns are widely documented \citep{davisIdentifyingFinancializationNonfinancial2016} and typically occur for diverse reasons, like better outside options from financial investment, a rise in the opportunity cost of productive
projects, or stakeholder demands for internal diversification to reduce perceived 
risk-factors following a standard portfolio approach. These factors would lead firms to reduce effective investment to secure available funds directing towards financial markets transactions
\citep{stockhammerFinancialisationSlowdownAccumulation2004}.

In the simplified variant captured below we retain the standard policy rule and the
exogenous credit-rationing parameter $c$, but allow asset prices to influence 
the investment-demand schedule via

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  I_p &= D, &
  D &= d_0-d_1r-d_2AP, \qquad d_2>0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:firms-ration}
\end{equation}

Thus a higher asset price lowers planned productive investment directly and, hence, does not require assuming any effect on the banking sector and the central bank as in the preceeding section \ref{subsec:central_bank}. The coefficient $d_2$ is a reduced-form measure of firms' sensitivity to financial valuations, representing the idea that firms strive for highly valued assets to create a credible portfolio and that financialization seems more attractive in times of booms.\footnote{I should be noted that such a line of reasoning leads to a scenario, where higher asset prices increase the relative attractiveness of assets running counter to the basic intuition of the liquidity preference, which implies that the motives driving contemporary financialization of firms are probably different from what Keynes' assumed about the behavior of rentier households roughly 100 years ago.}

This channel closes the real--financial loop directly. The interest rate affects
speculative borrowing and therefore $AP$; the resulting asset price enters
Eq.~\eqref{eq:firms-ration}, changes $D$, and feeds through to output and
employment. In the outcome we observe a feedback effect similar to that in section \ref{subsec:banks_ration} -- lower output and prices lead to lower interest rates that boost speculation. However, but this time the mechanism does not operate through endogenous bank credit rationing. Rather, as the label ``financialization of firms'' suggest firms effectively replace the role of banks as drivers of financzialization as they themselves gain a stronger preferences for holding asssets. As indicated in the bottom right panel of Figure \ref{fig:firmsration-equilibrium} this also leads to a greater share of speculative debt in total debt.

\section{Real world analysis: Interaction between speculation and policy} \label{sec:real_world}

In the following we use the framework and intuitions developed in the preceding section to tackle key interaction between speculation and economic policy. The first example adresses the obvious question emerging from the foregoing section, namely how to avoid or compensate for the dampening of speculation on real economic activities. We thereby propose an approach that employs discriminatory interest rate policies relative to speculative endeavors by subjecting the latter to an interest rate premium. This change can be analyzed within our basic modeling framework (see section \ref{subsec:dual_interest}) and, hence, provides in direct continuation of the presentation as found in section \ref{sec:real_impact}.

Finally, we switch perspectives in section \ref{subsec:speculation} further below and ask how (central bank) policy impacts in speculative activity providing a direct link between our modeling framework and the role of policy-induced changes in financial market developments that are often in focus of related discussions and media reporting.

\subsection{Dampening the impact of speculation by means of a dual interest rates} \label{subsec:dual_interest}

Building on the central bank  channel developed in Section \ref{subsec:central_bank}, we examine a specific policy scenario in which regulatory constraints lead banks to price asset-market credit differently from productive credit.
The underlying intuition is that of "dual interest rates". That means the monetary authority distiguishes between credit uses with the intent to keep credits for productive purposes cheap compared to credits for speculative assets.
Although concrete dual interest policies are not (yet) a established instrument in practice certain regulatory practices produce results that would be akin to this.

The oldest archetype is margin regulation in the United States: since the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, the Federal Reserve's Regulation~T has capped the share of a securities purchase that may be financed with borrowed funds (currently 50\%), and margin loans are priced at a premium over base rates, so that credit for securities purchases carries a systematically higher effective cost than credit for production.

China operates what is arguably the most explicit dual-rate system. Its banking system has long steered lending through a dual-track structure of policy and market rates \citep{heDualtrackInterestRates2012}, and under the mortgage-rate framework introduced in 2019, newly issued first-home mortgages faced a floor at the five-year LPR, while second-home mortgages faced a floor 60 basis points higher \citep{peoplesbankofchinaChinaReformIndividual2019}, i.e.\ a statutory $i_{AP}>1$.

Finally, sectoral capital requirements operate as a de facto $i_{AP}>1$: Belgium, for instance, has required banks to hold a sectoral systemic risk buffer of up to 9\% of CET1 capital against their IRB exposures to Belgian residential real estate since May 2022 \citep{nationalbankofbelgiumIntroductionSectoralSystemic2023}, raising the capital cost of mortgage lending relative to corporate lending without touching the general policy rate. Agent-based studies of borrower-specific credit constraints document exactly the mechanism our model emphasizes: restricting the credit access of investors---rather than tightening all credit uniformly---yields the strongest reductions in debt and cyclical house-price dynamics \citep{tarneEffectBorrowerspecificLoantovalue2022,tarneRoofRealEstate2025}.


Against this backdrop, we model the policy as follows. The productive rate $r$ is held unchanged.
By contrast, the lending rate applicable to speculative asset purchases, $r_{AP}$, is assumed to
be a constant multiple of $r$:
\[ r_{AP} = i_{AP} r, \]
where $i_{AP} > 1$ denotes the multiplier introduced through the regulatory policy.

Speculative demand is therefore given as
\begin{equation}
  SD = s_0 - s_1 r_{AP} - s_2 (AP - 1) = s_0 - s_1 i_{AP} r - s_2 (AP - 1).
\end{equation}

Thus, a higher $i_{AP}$ reduces speculative borrowing and, consequently, speculative debt. Because
the central bank channel is active in this scenario, the resulting decline in asset prices feeds
back into the policy rule (Eq.~\ref{eq:i-ap}): the lending rate $r$ falls, which lowers financing
costs for productive investment relative to the central-bank scenario of
Section~\ref{subsec:central_bank}. At the same time, lower speculative demand depresses the asset
price itself, given the fixed supply of assets (Eq.~\ref{eq:as}); the effect is visible in the
asset price of $AP = 0.18$, compared to $0.87$ in the central-bank scenario.

This mechanism is reflected in Figure~\ref{fig:pqcrdiff-equilibrium}: for $i_{AP} = 2$, the risk
indicator $\psi$ falls from $0.10$ to $0.02$, while GDP rises from $6.32$ to $6.51$.

\subsection{Financial market dynamics as decisions under uncertainty}\label{subsec:uncertainty}

The mechanisms captured by our model variants are not merely theoretical artefacts. The period 2020--2023 provides a useful illustration of the real-financial nexus, although some of the empirical channels extend beyond the deliberately sparse teaching model.

\paragraph{Quantitative easing and the ``everything rally.''}
Between March 2020 and early 2022, the Federal Reserve purchased roughly \$1.33~trillion in mortgage-backed securities---nearly 90\% of the growth in eligible mortgages \citep{klein2025}. Combined with near-zero interest rates and comparable programmes at other major central banks, this produced what market participants called an ``everything rally'': equities, bonds, real estate, commodities, and crypto-assets all rose in synchrony \citep{ft2020everything, handwiki}. The Brookings Institution estimates that US home values rose by approximately \$100,000 on average, adding \$12~trillion in household equity and generating \$480--840~billion in additional consumption through the housing wealth effect \citep{klein2025}. The first part of this sequence is represented by Eqs.~\ref{eq:sd}--\ref{eq:ap}: a lower lending rate raises speculative debt, gross asset demand, and the asset price. The observed wealth effect is not present in the teaching variants, whose real feedbacks instead operate through credit rationing, the policy rate, or firms' investment demand; adding consumption out of wealth would require a further equation.

\paragraph{Monetary tightening and the ``crypto winter.''}
When the Fed began raising rates in March 2022, the effect was felt immediately in the most leveraged corner of the financial circulation. An IMF study of the ``crypto cycle'' finds that a one-percentage-point rise in the shadow federal funds rate produces a persistent 0.15 standard-deviation decline in the common factor driving crypto-asset prices, with the entire market losing over \$1.7~trillion in value from its November 2021 peak \citep{cheCryptoCycleUS2023}. The authors attribute this to the risk-taking channel: tighter monetary policy raises the effective risk aversion of the marginal investor, who responds by deleveraging. This maps onto the asset block common to all variants: rising \(i \to\) rising \(r \to\) falling \(SD\) \(\to\) falling \(AE \to\) falling \(AP\). PQCr adds the reverse contemporaneous link, from \(AP\) to the policy rate, whereas PQCrDIFF strengthens the direct cost of speculative borrowing through \(r_{AP}=i_{AP}r\).

The fact that the post-2020 correlation between crypto and equity markets rose sharply, and that the sensitivity of crypto prices to monetary policy became significant only after institutional investors entered the market \citep{cheCryptoCycleUS2023}, is also instructive. It suggests that the ``turnover multiplier'' \(1/(1-\gamma)\) advanced in our model is not a fixed structural parameter but a function of institutional participation---as more institutional investors trade crypto, the effective turnover rate \(\gamma\) rises, the multiplier increases, and the sensitivity of asset demand to changes in speculative debt grows.

\paragraph{The two-way street.}
Our model emphasizes the arrow from monetary conditions to asset prices, but recent experience also illustrates the reverse: the housing wealth effect from QE generated additional consumption, contributing to the post-2021 inflation that forced the Fed to tighten in the first place \citep{klein2025}. This creates a closed loop that our static model cannot fully capture but that the dynamic extensions of the \texttt{PKAssetPrices} framework are designed to explore: easy money \(\to\) rising asset prices \(\to\) rising consumption \(\to\) inflation \(\to\) tighter money \(\to\) falling asset prices. Keynes's insight that the speculative motive connects financial and industrial circulations in \emph{both} directions is borne out by the data.

\section{Conclusion}

The distance from the \treatise{} to the \GT{} is often described as a profound theoretical rupture---the shift from the quantity theory of money (via the fundamental equations) to the principle of effective demand. Our exercise suggests a more parsimonious interpretation: in the limited domain of the real-financial nexus, the two books differ along two independent dimensions---the nature of money and the connectedness of speculation to the real economy---and each occupies a different cell in the resulting 2\,$\times$\,2 typology.

The contrast in the \GT[Ch.~15] is instructive. Having spent years elaborating the deposit-classification system of the \treatise{}, Keynes tells us in a single sentence that the three deposit categories form ``a single pool... [with] no necessity to segregate into three water-tight compartments.'' This is not a minor terminological adjustment. It is the surrender of the \treatise{}'s central analytical device---the isolation of the financial circulation---in favour of a framework in which speculation and enterprise draw on the same pool of liquidity. But it is also, simultaneously, the surrender of the \treatise{}'s sophisticated credit-money apparatus in favour of an analytically simpler model where the money stock is given.

Our model makes this visible. The Baseline variant reproduces the \treatise{} result: pure balance-sheet inflation in an endogenous money framework. Adding Eq.~(\ref{eq:c-sd}) or Eq.~(\ref{eq:i-ap}) closes the speculation--real-economy loop while retaining endogenous money, placing us in the bottom-right cell of our typology. The turn of a single equation separates the two books along the second dimension; the first dimension (endogenous vs.\ exogenous money) is a more fundamental choice about model architecture.

The recent empirical record---from the everything rally of 2020--2021 to the crypto winter of 2022--2023, from the housing wealth effect of QE to the leveraged deleveraging triggered by rate hikes---suggests that the bottom-right cell (endogenous money with real feedback from speculation) is the empirically relevant one for the contemporary world. But the \treatise{} distinction between industrial and financial circulation remains analytically useful: it forces us to specify the \emph{exact channel} through which the financial circulation reaches the industrial circulation, rather than treating the connection as automatic. The distinction between the PQC (credit crowding-out) and PQCr (interest rate) channels matters for policy: macroprudential tools versus interest rate policy are not perfect substitutes, as recent debates on ``leaning against the wind'' illustrate.

This parsimony is itself a pedagogical lesson: large theoretical differences can turn on small structural assumptions. The space of possibilities defined by our four cells also reminds us that the choice between the \treatise{} and the \GT{} is not forced---a third position (our bottom-right) combines the descriptive richness of the former with the causal mechanism of the latter, and is arguably the most adequate framework for understanding the contemporary world.
